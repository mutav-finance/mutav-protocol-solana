# Showing protocol mechanics in demos, two-sided B2B stories, and honest disclosure

Research note for MUTAV's ≤3-minute demo. Gathered 2026-10-11 with web search and page fetches.

**Evidence caveat (read first):** Search did not reliably find specific demo *videos* from the named protocols (Huma, Maple, Centrifuge, Credix, Ondo, OnRe, Nexus Mutual, Etherisc, Neptune Mutual, Sphere, Bridge). Where a technique is described below without a video link, it comes from written product docs or general demo guidance, not from a watched demo. The rules on disclosure and hackathon judging are well sourced from primary pages. The video-technique sections are thinner, and several patterns are marked as inferences.

## 1. Techniques for showing state and money moving (explorer proof, live balances, before/after, split screens, tx callouts, timestamps)

### Takeaway
Colosseum asks the technical demo to show "Solana integration, on-chain logic, and overall architecture", and says "architecture visuals" and interface walkthroughs are acceptable. So proof of on-chain state belongs in the demo video, not the pitch. The reliable pattern in the docs below is a status-driven lifecycle (pending → approved → executed) that ends in an explorer-checkable transaction. Backend demos generally open with an architecture diagram so the viewer knows which component is on screen.

### Cited Findings
- Colosseum's technical demo video is "2-3 minutes" and covers "the how", not the why. Judges are "especially interested in 'Solana integration, on-chain logic, and overall architecture'". Acceptable formats include "interface walkthroughs, or architecture visuals". One listed mistake is "Failing to explain Solana integration clearly." — [Colosseum blog: Perfecting Your Hackathon Submission](https://blog.colosseum.com/perfecting-your-hackathon-submission/)
- Colosseum's official FAQ requires "A product-demo video of no more than three minutes explaining how the product works", separate from a "two-to-three-minute presentation video". — [Colosseum hackathon page](https://colosseum.com/hackathon)
- The Squads multisig flow is a status lifecycle with no hidden steps. A transaction is queued and shows as pending under Transactions. Each signer approves until the threshold is met, and the status changes from "Active" to "Ready for Execution". Clicking Execute sets the status to "Executed", and the transaction "can then be checked on a Solana explorer". — [QuickNode guide: Multisig with Squads](https://www.quicknode.com/guides/solana-development/3rd-party-integrations/multisig-with-squads); [Squads docs: Approve Proposal](https://docs.squads.so/main/development/typescript/instructions/approve-proposal)
- Squads' developer quickstart demonstrates the whole lifecycle with a minimal, concrete payload: create the multisig, create a proposal, vote with two keypairs, then execute a 0.1 SOL transfer. — [Squads quickstart](https://docs.squads.so/main/v/development/introduction/quickstart)
- Backend-demo guidance (via a search summary; the page did not render when fetched): a backend demo has no natural UI, so the presenter has to make the system visible. Start with an overall architecture diagram so the audience can see which component is being shown at each moment. — [How To Present Backend Demos (Notion)](https://rxhl.notion.site/How-To-Present-Backend-Demos-861303c09e3e48fcb33a2a6999260e59) (snippet only; not verified in full)
- Circle teaches CCTP through a code walkthrough video series rather than a dual-explorer view. A Circle-ecosystem workshop on Plume ended by verifying the cross-chain transfer "on both explorers". This is the closest example I found of a two-explorer proof shot, and I found no recording. — [Circle: Moving USDC securely between blockchains](https://circle.com/fr/developer/walkthroughs-tutorials/moving-usdc-securely-between-blockchains---an-introduction-using-cctp-1); [Luma: CCTP + USDC on Plume workshop](https://luma.com/lf4lgx8p)
- Nexus Mutual's own docs explicitly avoid screen-by-screen walkthroughs because "screens change more often than the process". Their claims material explains the process instead: proof of loss on-chain, a 0.05 ETH claim deposit, and Claims Committee assessment. — [Nexus Mutual docs: Using](https://docs.nexusmutual.io/using/); [File a claim](https://docs.nexusmutual.io/using/file-a-claim); [Claims assessment](https://docs.nexusmutual.io/protocol/claims-assessment)
- A judging-criteria breakdown says high-scoring demos show real data, not placeholder text. It lists "hardcoded everything", meaning the project only works with preloaded data, among low-scoring patterns. — [Reskilll: What Hackathon Judges Look For](https://blogs.reskilll.com/what-hackathon-judges-look-for-complete-judging-criteria-breakdown-2026/) (secondary blog)

### Inferences
- For MUTAV, each on-chain step works best as a visible status chip that changes in the app ("Registered on-chain ✓", "Claim paid ✓", "PIX recorded ✓"). Pair each chip with a short signature callout (first and last 4 characters plus a "view on Solana Explorer (devnet)" link). Then cut once to the explorer showing the same signature and the cluster=devnet badge. One explorer cut is enough proof. More than one turns the demo into a code walkthrough.
- A persistent reserve meter (BRS held vs. required coverage, with the coverage ratio) is the natural "live balance" device. Showing it change on registration (required coverage rises) and on claim payment (reserve falls) makes the money visible without reading accounts.
- Timestamps on the claim timeline (filed hh:mm:ss → paid → PIX recorded) are the cheapest way to back up "within minutes". An on-screen elapsed clock does the same job.
- Opening with a 5–10 second architecture strip (Agency app → Operator → Solana program/reserve → PIX) and highlighting the active box as the story moves follows the backend-demo advice above. It also answers Colosseum's "overall architecture" ask.

### Gaps
- I could not find or watch specific demo videos from Huma, Maple, Centrifuge, Credix, Ondo, OnRe, Nexus Mutual, Etherisc, Neptune Mutual, Sphere, Bridge or Circle that use split-screen or live-meter techniques. Search returned docs and profile pages instead. The writer should not attribute specific on-screen techniques to these brands.
- I found no source quantifying how judges react to explorer cutaways versus in-app proof.

## 2. Dramatizing a refusal or safety guarantee (something the system prevents)

### Takeaway
I found no demo-specific source on staging a refusal. The best primary analogue is Stripe Radar: a block is a first-class, named outcome (`blocked`) with a plain-language reason ("Stripe blocked this charge as too risky"). It is distinct from an issuer decline, and test inputs can trigger it deterministically. That suggests treating MUTAV's coverage-gate refusal as a designed, labeled outcome with a stated reason, triggered on purpose.

### Cited Findings
- A Radar block is Stripe's own decision, separate from an issuer decline. Stripe does not seek authorization, and the outcome is typed "blocked" with a seller message such as "Stripe blocked this charge as too risky". — [Stripe docs: Declines](https://docs.stripe.com/declines)
- Stripe provides deterministic test triggers: a highest-risk test charge is always blocked regardless of your rules, and rules can be evaluated against historical payments. — [Stripe docs: Radar testing](https://stripe.com/en-dk/docs/radar/testing)
- The Squads threshold is a "prevention" shown by state. A proposal cannot execute until approvals equal the threshold, and the UI shows it as not yet "Ready for Execution". — [Squads docs: Approve Proposal](https://docs.squads.so/main/development/typescript/instructions/approve-proposal); [QuickNode guide](https://www.quicknode.com/guides/solana-development/3rd-party-integrations/multisig-with-squads)
- General hackathon advice: judges penalize a demo that never reaches its payoff more than one that skips a live call, so teams mock flaky external services and prepare fallbacks. — [HackerEarth: 10 tips to win a hackathon](https://www.hackerearth.com/blog/10-tips-win-hackathon) (secondary blog)

### Inferences
- Structure the refusal as a three-beat "try → refused with reason → state unchanged" sequence. (1) Show the meter near the limit, e.g. "coverage 0.10 required; 1 more guarantee of R$X would breach it". (2) The operator attempts to register and the program rejects it, shown as a named error ("CoverageExceeded: required coverage would exceed reserve") rendered in the app, not a stack trace. (3) The meter is unchanged and the explorer shows a failed transaction or simulation. The unchanged state is the proof that the guarantee held.
- Set it up before it happens: state the rule in one sentence first ("the reserve can never promise more than it holds"). The refusal then plays as the system keeping its promise, not as a bug. Contrast it with an accepted registration just before, so viewers see the same action succeed and then fail.
- Trigger it deterministically: pre-seed the devnet reserve near its limit, the way Stripe uses always-blocked test inputs, so the refusal is reliable on camera.
- Name the refusal the way Radar does: a first-class outcome type plus a human-readable reason. That shows it is designed behavior.

### Gaps
- I found no recorded crypto protocol demo (insurance or credit) that stages a coverage refusal on screen. No source covers judge reactions to "negative path" demos specifically.

## 3. Two-sided B2B demos (end-customer surface and the infrastructure in one story)

### Takeaway
Infrastructure companies tell two-sided stories with a concrete fictional business on the platform side and its users on the other. Stripe's examples are Rocket Rides (a marketplace connecting pilots with riders) and a pet-salon platform paying fees from the Stripe balance at Sessions. The infrastructure appears as what makes that business's moments work. For MUTAV, that means one named fictional agency, one tenant/landlord case, and the protocol shown at the moments it acts.

### Cited Findings
- Stripe's canonical Connect demo, Rocket Rides, is a fictional on-demand marketplace connecting independent pilots with customers. It demonstrates onboarding and account management for connected accounts, with full source on GitHub, and Stripe recommends trying it before building. — [Stripe docs: Express accounts](https://docs.stripe.com/connect/express-accounts); [Stripe: SaaS platforms and marketplaces](https://docs.stripe.com/connect/saas-platforms-and-marketplaces)
- At Stripe Sessions 2025, developer-session presenters built a fictional pet-salon platform and paid subscription fees directly from the Stripe balance to show on-network money movement. The source does not say whether it was live. — [Frontier Fintech newsletter on Stripe Sessions 2025](https://frontierfintech.substack.com/p/82-step-by-step-ferociously-stripe) (secondary; surfaced via search summary)
- Stripe announced stablecoin financial accounts at Sessions 2025 (May 6–8, San Francisco). They let businesses hold balances and pay international suppliers, using Circle's USDC and Bridge's USDB. This shows Stripe pitching stablecoin infrastructure through a business use case, not chain mechanics. — [Highlights from Stripe Sessions 2025](https://hostmerchantservices.com/2025/06/stripe-sessions-2025/); [NBC: Stripe closes Bridge deal](https://www.nbcsandiego.com/news/business/money-report/stripe-closes-1-1-billion-bridge-deal-prepares-for-aggressive-stablecoin-push/3743860/?amp=1)
- The Squads UI shows the multi-actor story inside one product: a second member connects their wallet to approve. The "other side" is shown as a second actor taking a turn. — [QuickNode guide](https://www.quicknode.com/guides/solana-development/3rd-party-integrations/multisig-with-squads)
- Colosseum frames hackathons as "startup competitions" judged on Founder-Market Fit, Insight, Product + Execution, Market Size, Founder Communication, Viability and Traction. A customer-facing story is therefore scored, not decoration. — [Colosseum hackathon page](https://colosseum.com/hackathon)

### Inferences
- Use one persistent fictional cast, the Rocket Rides approach: e.g. "Imobiliária Exemplo" (agency), one tenant, one landlord. Every protocol action is a consequence of something that happens to them: lease signed → guarantee registered, rent missed → claim filed → landlord paid via PIX.
- A split screen (agency app left, protocol/reserve panel or explorer right) fits the moments when both sides change at once: registration and claim payment. Full-screen app for the customer beats and full-screen panel for the refusal is likely clearer at 3 minutes than a split screen held throughout. Small video frames make split-screen text unreadable.
- Because the agency platform predates the hackathon and is not wired to the chain, the two-sided story should be cut honestly. Show the agency surface as context ("this is the agency product, built before the hackathon"). Then make the hackathon-built operator → program → reserve path the part that visibly acts.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve a Bridge, Plaid, Sphere or Squads *video* showing customer and infrastructure views together. The patterns above come from docs and secondary event coverage.

## 4. Honesty: pre-existing code, simulated parts, devnet status, and what judges penalize

### Takeaway
Colosseum explicitly allows pre-existing code if "all relevant past development work" is disclosed in the submission form. Only work between the start and end dates is judged, and misrepresenting development history can lead to disqualification. ETHGlobal's norm is similar: "clearly document pre-existing work" and "clearly distinguish between what's new and what's reused". Disclosure is the rule. Concealment, not prior work, is what gets penalized.

### Cited Findings
- Colosseum: "Builders may use pre-existing code, but teams must disclose all relevant past development work in the submission form." Open-source code by others doesn't count as pre-existing. Judging covers only work done between the start and end dates. Misrepresenting development history can lead to disqualification. Reviewers look for significant work done during the hackathon by the team itself. — [Colosseum hackathon page (FAQ)](https://colosseum.com/hackathon)
- Colosseum targets "new startups that haven't raised significant outside capital." — [Colosseum hackathon page](https://colosseum.com/hackathon)
- An unofficial Colosseum tips/FAQ page (Superteam), via search snippet, reports a past-season rule: development no more than 2 weeks before the start, with pre-existing ideas welcome. It conflicts with the current official FAQ above, which allows disclosed pre-existing code. Treat the official page as authoritative. — [Superteam Colosseum Tips (Notion)](https://superteamcan.notion.site/Colosseum-Tips-1ae380b97c4681cabad4e7f85ae0af7b) (page did not render on fetch; snippet only)
- ETHGlobal Classic track: "all work on your project must begin after the hackathon officially starts". Prior project-specific code is not allowed except public libraries or starter kits. Prior projects may enter but lose partner prizes and Finalist eligibility. Continuity tracks: "Continuity submissions must clearly document pre-existing work and include new features or functionality developed during the hackathon." Also: "Clearly distinguish between what's new and what's reused", and "Submissions with large single commits or missing histories may be disqualified." — [ETHGlobal Cannes rules](https://ethglobal.com/events/cannes/info/details)
- ETHGlobal demo-video rules: 2–4 minutes; no sped-up footage; no music-plus-text in place of narration; no text-to-speech or AI voiceover; no phone recording; at least 720p. AI use must be documented. — [ETHGlobal Cannes rules](https://ethglobal.com/events/cannes/info/details)
- Other events' variants: ETHRome requires stating in the submission that you're working on an existing project, and only post-start commits are considered. Sui requires prior approval for pre-existing code. Huddle01 judges pre-existing projects at judges' discretion. — [ETHRome Hackathon Rules](https://ethrome25.notion.site/Hackathon-Rules-160d00c099af8156b330c0bac363c3ed); [search summary of Sui/Huddle01 rules](https://huddle01.notion.site/Guidelines-Criteria-c5104bf0e58e4906aad12a8e4d2f29ec)
- Colosseum lists these pitch-video mistakes: exceeding 3 minutes, "overly flashy visuals with little substance", buzzwords, vague or overly technical explanations, omitting team background. "A clear and well-structured narrative is more valuable than professional video editing." — [Colosseum blog](https://blog.colosseum.com/perfecting-your-hackathon-submission/)
- Faked live demos and videos have long been named as a hackathon cheating pattern (2013 MHacks discussion; informal, not a rule). — [Slashdot: Preventing Cheating at Hackathons](https://developers.slashdot.org/story/13/09/16/143218/preventing-cheating-at-hackathons/informative-comments)
- Mocking external services to guarantee the demo reaches its payoff is commonly recommended. "Hardcoded everything" (works only with preloaded data) is scored low. — [HackerEarth tips](https://www.hackerearth.com/blog/10-tips-win-hackathon); [Reskilll judging breakdown](https://blogs.reskilll.com/what-hackathon-judges-look-for-complete-judging-criteria-breakdown-2026/) (secondary blogs)
- Colosseum Accelerator material describes cohorts progressing "from devnet to mainnet". Devnet is a normal stage in Colosseum's world, not a weakness in itself. — [Introducing Colosseum Accelerator Cohort 1](https://blog.colosseum.com/introducing-colosseum-accelerator-cohort-1/)

### Inferences
- MUTAV's disclosure set, consistent with the rules above, should cover three items. (a) The agency B2B platform predates the hackathon and is not yet wired to the chain: say so in the submission form (required by Colosseum) and once on screen. (b) The PIX transfer is simulated on devnet, while its settlement record on-chain is real. (c) It runs on devnet. These can be compact on-screen labels, not apologies: a small persistent "devnet" badge, a "simulated PIX (devnet)" tag on the PIX step, and a "built before the hackathon" caption on the agency screens. A "Built during hackathon / Pre-existing / Simulated / Roadmap" legend on the architecture strip is a single, honest way to cover all of them.
- Pair each disclosure with what is real. For example: "PIX transfer simulated on devnet; the settlement record you see is a real on-chain transaction." That keeps the story strong while staying accurate.
- Put roadmap items (agency platform ↔ chain wiring, real PIX rails, mainnet) in one closing "next" beat, visually distinct from the demo footage, so nothing on the roadmap appears to be shipped.
- Penalty risk is concentrated in concealment and history, not prior work. Things to avoid: implying the agency app is wired on-chain; showing a PIX "payment" without the simulated label; a repo with large single commits or missing history (an explicit ETHGlobal disqualifier, and a likely red flag for Colosseum's "misrepresenting development history").
- Production norms to follow even though Colosseum doesn't state them: real voice narration and no sped-up footage (ETHGlobal rules). Cut waiting time with visible jump-cuts and a timestamp, not speed-ramping, especially since "within minutes" is a claim.

### Gaps
- Colosseum's public pages say nothing about labeling simulated or mocked components, or about devnet vs. mainnet requirements for submissions. I found no statement either way.
- No primary source gives judges' views on disclosed mocks specifically. The inference that disclosed mocks are accepted rests on general rules (disclosure plus judging only in-window work) and informal blog advice.
- The exact current Colosseum submission-form wording for "past development work" could not be retrieved beyond the FAQ text.
