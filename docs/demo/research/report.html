Show the refusal, then show the payout

MUTAV's demo should be a technical walkthrough of one lease in one take: one end-to-end flow, a staged refusal, visible on-chain proof, and honest labels. That is the pattern in winning Colosseum fintech demos and in professional product-video craft. A second pitch wastes the slot. Colosseum asks for a product-demo video of no more than three minutes that explains "how the product works". It sits next to a separate 2-to-3-minute pitch video. Colosseum's founders say the demo is about the how, and what judges care about most is "the reasoning behind these decisions, especially with regard to Solana integration" (Colosseum blog; colosseum.com/hackathon). Winning 2025 demos put product on screen within about 10 seconds, run one realistic counterparty flow, show a devnet signature, and say plainly what was shipped during the hackathon. The existing storyboard already has the right spine: Ana's lease registered, a second lease refused by the coverage gate, the claim filed, paid and settled. It needs four changes. First, add a short agency-platform shot and an architecture strip that labels what is pre-existing, what was built in the hackathon, and what is simulated. Second, cut the 21% flywheel beat, which is pitch material. Third, tighten the zoom and capture spec so the 200% punch-ins don't blur. Fourth, script the capture with Playwright and disposable devnet wallets so retakes are deterministic. Two process items: the judging criteria in the team's handoff brief are out of date, and the deadline needs one confirmation in the submission form.

Colosseum scores the demo on Product + Execution, not on the old rubric

The current Colosseum submission is a pitch to its venture fund and accelerator. The FAQ says the team wants to understand "founder-market fit, how the opportunity was uncovered, how the team prioritizes" (colosseum.com/hackathon). The official criteria are Founder + Market Fit, Insight, Product + Execution, Potential Market Size, Founder Communication, Viability and Traction. Product + Execution asks "How well does the product work? How does it stack up against the competition? How quickly is the team shipping?" Shortlisted teams get a 15-minute Zoom interview, and winners are announced about a month after the deadline (colosseum.com/hackathon). No weights are published. The team's internal handoff brief lists "Functionality, Impact, Novelty, UX, Open-source and Business Plan" as the official criteria. That list comes from 2024-era community docs such as a Superteam Canada Notion page (Superteam Canada) and is outdated. Plan against the current rubric. The demo carries Product + Execution most directly. It also carries Insight: the per-lease exposure registry plus the coverage gate is the mechanism nobody else has. Viability and Traction belong mainly to the pitch.

The two videos have distinct jobs. In the Breakout 2025 workshop, Nate Levine, Matty Taylor and Clay Robbins described the pitch as "the why" and the technical demo as "the how". The demo should cover core features, the tech stack, and why some components were prioritized over others. It must be "technical, direct, and specific to implementation", and teams should "avoid turning this into another pitch" (Colosseum blog). Their named mistakes include running over time, "overly flashy visuals with little substance," buzzwords, and failing to explain the Solana integration clearly. Matty Taylor's earlier advice still holds: prioritize the features that "create an amazing working demo on devnet" and build toward the user's "aha" moment (How to Win a Colosseum Hackathon). GitHub reviewers check that the team "did significant work during the hackathon" and "prioritized feature development strategically", not code style (colosseum.com/hackathon).

Disclosure is a rule, not a courtesy. "Builders may use pre-existing code, but teams must disclose all relevant past development work in the submission form." Only work between the start and end dates is judged, and misrepresentation can lead to disqualification (colosseum.com/hackathon). Devnet is normal. Colosseum's only technical requirement is to integrate with Solana "in some capacity," and its accelerator describes cohorts moving "from devnet to mainnet" (How to Win; Accelerator Cohort 1). No host or format is mandated. The Arena form has a separate demo-video link, a demoVideoPublic flag, and a live-product link with access instructions (Colosseum Copilot submission schema).

Deadline discrepancy, to resolve today. The colosseum.com page says only "Sep 14 — Oct 12" (colosseum.com/hackathon). Colosseum Copilot's event registry gives 2026-10-13T06:59Z. That is 03:59 BRT on Oct 13, which equals 23:59 Pacific on Oct 12. The storyboard and the team target Oct 12, 23:59 BRT, about four hours earlier than Copilot's timestamp. Keep the earlier target and confirm the cutoff in the Arena form itself. The run sheet's 20:30 submit and 23:00 hard stop leave buffer on either reading.

Professional craft: lead with the stakes, hold on results, cut every wait

Outside crypto, the best short product videos keep the real UI full-frame, show one job per video, and open on value. Linear's Head of Product narrates "Introducing Linear Initiatives" from a small webcam bubble while the real interface fills the frame. Linear's reels show "a single job to be done per reel" (Moonb). Figma's Motion launch puts the control and its effect in the same frame, so the viewer never has to remember what was clicked (Moonb). Stripe employs a dedicated Demo Engineer and builds believable fictional merchants for its keynotes, such as "GlowGuide" in 2025 (Stripe jobs; Stripe Sessions 2025). Its Rocket Rides marketplace is the canonical two-sided demo cast (Stripe docs). Peter Cohan's Great Demo! rule, "Do the Last Thing First," and Guy Kawasaki's "don't try building to a crescendo" both argue for front-loading the strongest result (SK Murphy). Supademo says the first 10 seconds should state the viewer's problem, not introduce the product (Supademo).

Arcade's 2026 benchmarks (14 million interactive sessions) give the only hard numbers. They come from click-through demos, not linear video, so treat them as directional. Completion peaks at 40% for 9–12 steps. Step 7 is the main drop-off, and viewers who pass it are 2.3× likelier to finish. On-screen labels over 60 characters cut completion 12%, and specific numbers beat vague claims (Arcade). Translated to a 3-minute film: the first 45–60 seconds are the decision window. Aim for about a dozen visible actions, not 25. Keep every caption chip short.

Screen polish is mostly discipline. "Lots of mouse movement can make the viewer feel disoriented": move with purpose, then park the cursor (Consensus). Callouts, zooms and pans beat pointing, and the edit order is to cut dead air before adding effects (TechSmith). Record one capability per take, at a frame slightly larger than the app, with notifications off (Consensus). Narration runs at about 140–150 wpm (Bread n Beyond), so a 3:00 video holds roughly 420 words at most, and fewer if results need room to land. Burn in open captions, because publishers report up to 85% of feed video plays muted (Digiday). Don't narrate what judges can already see; add context instead (AngelHack). On voice, Arcade measured 14% higher completion with AI voiceover in interactive demos, and >55% completion when a demo opens with a personal camera intro (Arcade). ETHGlobal bans text-to-speech narration and sped-up footage outright (ETHGlobal rules). Colosseum has no such rule, but a founder's real voice is the lower-risk choice for a trust product. Disclose a cloned voice if one is used.

Make the coverage gate visible as designed behavior, not a failure

No recorded crypto credit or guarantee demo in this research staged a coverage refusal on screen. That gap is the opportunity. The closest primary analogue is Stripe Radar. A block is a first-class outcome typed blocked, with a plain-language reason ("Stripe blocked this charge as too risky"). It is distinct from an issuer decline, and deterministic test inputs always trigger it (Stripe Declines; Radar testing). Squads shows prevention through state: a proposal cannot reach "Ready for Execution" until approvals meet the threshold (QuickNode). For MUTAV, the refusal works in three beats. State the rule. Show the same action that just succeeded now fail with a named reason. Then show that the state is unchanged. Pre-seed the reserve so the refusal is deterministic, not lucky. The storyboard already does all of this in F6–F7: the red preview replays the program's rule, InsufficientFreeCapital is shown by name, and the note "Nothing was written" confirms state. It is the strongest Insight beat in the submission.

For money movement, the backend-demo rule is to show the architecture first, so viewers know which component is on screen (How To Present Backend Demos, snippet only). Colosseum names "architecture visuals" as acceptable demo content (Colosseum blog). A judging breakdown scores demos higher for real data and lower for "hardcoded everything" (Reskilll). The devices that follow are design judgement, not sourced findings. A before/after table under each confirmed transaction. A status chip with a truncated signature. One cut to Solana Explorer with the devnet badge. Timestamps on the claim row (filed → paid → settled) to back the "within minutes" claim. The storyboard's "How the reserve moved" tables and the /reserve#claims duration columns are exactly these devices. The one thing missing is an architecture strip that answers "why is this on-chain?" before the mechanics start. The answer is that reserve math and the coverage gate are enforced by the program, while personal data stays off-chain.

Tell the two-sided story honestly: label what is old, new, simulated and next

Infrastructure companies sell the platform through one named fictional business. For Stripe that is Rocket Rides, and at Sessions 2025 a pet-salon platform paying fees from its Stripe balance (Stripe docs; Frontier Fintech). MUTAV's cast is already right: Ana, Imobiliária Exemplo, Passo Fundo. What's missing is the agency side of the product. The storyboard never shows the agency platform, so a judge sees an operator console and a public reserve page, and nothing the agency uses. One short shot of the agency platform, labeled honestly, turns "a reserve program" into "a B2B guarantee product with a protocol underneath." The protocol then appears exactly where it acts.

Honesty here is a strength, provided each disclosure is paired with what is real. MCPay and Yumi disclosed prior work inside the video. Autonom separated pre-hackathon deployments from its new Solana work (MCPay demo; Yumi demo). ETHGlobal's continuity rules ask teams to "clearly distinguish between what's new and what's reused," and flag "large single commits or missing histories" for possible disqualification (ETHGlobal rules). Mocking external services so a demo reaches its payoff is accepted practice, as long as it is disclosed (HackerEarth). MUTAV needs three labels, each kept short:

  • The agency platform: "built before the hackathon · not yet wired on-chain".
  • The PIX step: "Devnet: PIX transfer simulated · settlement record on-chain". The storyboard already has this caption in F10.
  • The network: a persistent devnet badge.

A single legend on the architecture strip (built during the hackathon / pre-existing / simulated / next) covers all three in one frame. The submission form's prior-work and repoContext fields should mirror the video exactly. The public /reserve page does not say the PIX was simulated, so the submission text must.

Production toolchain: script the capture, render the cards, keep the voice human

Capture. Playwright's built-in recordVideo defaults to fitting 800×800 and exposes no quality or fps settings (Playwright docs). A GitHub issue reports Chromium's recorder bitrate hard-coded at about 1 Mbit/s (playwright#31424). That is acceptable for a rough cut and soft on text-heavy dashboards. The 2026 demo-as-code pattern takes a CDP screencast or a screenshot per frame, logs every click's bounding box to JSON, then composites a vector cursor and an eased zoom camera in a second pass. See programatic-demo (Playwright + Remotion, a hold of at least 1.3 s per zoom) and demo-video-skill (Playwright + Pillow + ffmpeg at 60 fps, where reframing is a re-render, not a re-shoot). For signing, it is more reliable to inject a Wallet-Standard test wallet holding a disposable devnet keypair via addInitScript than to automate the Phantom extension. That is a judgement call, since no authoritative Phantom-automation source was found. Splice in one hand-recorded real Phantom approval for texture. Squads rounds signed with Ledger stay manual and off-camera. Wait on UI state ("Confirmed"), not fixed sleeps. Keep the keypair devnet-only and out of git.

Zoom and cards. GUI tools like Screen Studio auto-zoom only from their own cursor telemetry. On imported footage they lose cursor smoothing (Screen Studio guide), so drive zoom from the script's click log instead. A 1.5–2× punch-in on a 1080p capture shows upscaled pixels. Capture at deviceScaleFactor: 2, or keep zoom at 1.3× or below. For title cards and the architecture strip, HyperFrames (Apache 2.0, HTML + GSAP, official Claude Code plugin, deterministic MP4 via headless Chrome and FFmpeg) is the lowest-friction agent-authored option (hyperframes). Remotion is free for companies of up to three employees (Remotion license). On a fanless laptop, run renders serially at concurrency 2–3 and never alongside test suites.

Voice, captions, assembly, hosting. Record the founder's voice to the rough cut. If a clone is needed for regeneration, ElevenLabs Professional Voice Cloning requires live verification and the Creator plan, and fine-tuning takes hours (Proof News). Generate captions from the script's own text, with Whisper used only for timing, and prompt it with "MUTAV, PIX, BRS, CPF". Ship an SRT as well as burned-in captions. Assemble in ffmpeg and finish with a two-pass loudnorm at about −14 LUFS and −1 dBTP. Sources split between −14 and −16 (dev.to; video-use). Keep a timeline.json so the ≤3:00 limit is a computed check. Upload to YouTube as unlisted at least an hour early, check it in a logged-out window, and keep the MP4 as a backup.

Conclusion

The research changes what "compelling" means for this entry. Winners did not out-polish anyone. Polished-but-empty is a named failure mode, and slide-only pitches still won in 2026. Winners made one claim checkable: a payment "4 seconds ago" in the explorer, a signed multisig, a real checkout. MUTAV has a stronger checkable claim than most, because its signature moment is a refusal that leaves state unchanged, paired with a payout the same rule never blocks. Few demos show what a protocol won't do. That is the Insight a guarantor should lead with, and it fits inside a minute.

The biggest risk is not production quality but ambiguity about what is real. Two things would trigger the misrepresentation clause or quietly cost Product + Execution credit: an agency platform that looks wired to the chain, or a PIX that looks like it moved money. The architecture-strip legend and the "what's next" card turn that risk into evidence of disciplined scoping. That speaks directly to "how the team prioritizes." Confirm the cutoff in the Arena form today, and update the handoff brief's rubric so the pitch video is also built against the current criteria.
